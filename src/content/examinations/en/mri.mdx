<KeyFacts>
- MRI uses a magnetic field and radio waves. There is no X-ray radiation.
- It shows ligaments, menisci, cartilage, nerves and swelling inside bone — none of which an X-ray shows.
- That detail has a cost: abnormalities turn up very often in people with no symptoms.
- You lie still in a noisy tube for 20 to 60 minutes.
- Always tell the staff about metal or implanted electronic devices.
</KeyFacts>

## How it works

MRI stands for magnetic resonance imaging. The machine creates a very strong magnetic field and sends radio waves through it. Water molecules in different tissues respond differently, and a computer assembles the returning signals into cross-sectional images, slice by slice.

Because soft tissues differ in water content, MRI can tell ligament from muscle from meniscus from cartilage — which an X-ray cannot do.

## What it shows that an X-ray does not

- Ligaments such as the ACL, and tendons such as the rotator cuff
- The menisci of the knee, and the discs of the spine
- Joint cartilage directly
- <Glossary definition="fluid within the bone under a joint surface, often after injury or unusual loading">**Bone marrow oedema**</Glossary>, which is sometimes an important clue
- Inflammation, masses and infection at a stage where an X-ray is still normal

## The most important limitation — it finds too much

This is worth knowing before you go.

<Callout type="warning" title="Abnormal MRI findings are common in people with no symptoms">
One study scanned 230 knees in 115 adults who had **no knee pain and no history of injury**, using a high-resolution 3.0 tesla machine. Abnormalities were found in 97% of the knees. Meniscal tears were present in 30% of them.

Put another way: take people off the street whose knees feel fine, scan them, and almost every one will have "something" in the report, with nearly a third showing a meniscal tear.

This does not make MRI useless. It means **what is on the scan has to be read alongside your symptoms**. A finding may be the cause of your problem, or it may be a trace of ordinary use that has been there for years without ever causing trouble.
</Callout>

That is why systematic reviews of high-quality guidelines recommend using imaging **selectively** — when the result will change a decision, rather than for reassurance.

Also mention a joint replacement, plates or screws, large or very recent tattoos, medication patches, or pregnancy. Most modern metal implants are safe in an MRI scanner, but that is a judgement for the staff to make, not something to guess at.

## What the scan is like

You lie on a table that slides into the tube, with the body part being scanned at the centre of the magnet. The machine makes loud rhythmic knocking sounds. You will be given ear protection and a call button.

The most important thing is to **stay still**. Even small movements blur the images enough to make them unreadable, and the sequence has to be repeated.

If small spaces frighten you, say so when the scan is booked rather than on the day. There is a range of things that help, from a careful explanation of what will happen through to medication if your doctor thinks it appropriate.

## Questions to ask your doctor

<DoctorChecklist>
- Why this scan now, and how will the result change the plan?
- Is what was found the cause of my symptoms, or something common for my age?
- If I do not have the scan now, what are the alternatives?
- Do I need contrast, and why?
- I have metal or a device implanted — what do I need to do?
</DoctorChecklist>
