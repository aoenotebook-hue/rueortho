<KeyFacts>
- DXA is the standard test for measuring bone density.
- Very low radiation dose, painless, and takes about 10 to 20 minutes.
- The sites measured are usually the lumbar spine and the hip.
- The result is a T-score, compared against healthy young adults.
- The number is only part of it — fracture risk also depends on age, past fractures and risk of falling.
</KeyFacts>

## What DXA is

DXA stands for dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. The machine sends X-rays at two energy levels through the bone and measures how much is absorbed. Denser bone absorbs more. From that it calculates the mineral density of the bone.

You lie on your back while an arm passes slowly over you. There is no tunnel, no loud noise and no long breath-holding.

## T-score and Z-score

**T-score** compares your bone density with the average for healthy young adults, in units of standard deviation. This is the value used to diagnose osteoporosis in postmenopausal women and older men.

**Z-score** compares you with people of your own age and sex. It is used in younger adults, children and premenopausal women, where comparison with young adults would not be informative.

| T-score | Standard interpretation |
|---|---|
| −1.0 or above | bone density in the normal range |
| between −1.0 and −2.5 | low bone mass (osteopenia) |
| −2.5 or below | meets the criterion for osteoporosis |

<Callout type="info" title="The number is not the whole diagnosis">
Clinical practice guidelines for postmenopausal osteoporosis are clear that the diagnosis does not rest on the T-score alone. Someone who has already broken a hip in a low-energy fall, or has a vertebral compression fracture, may be diagnosed with osteoporosis even if the T-score has not reached −2.5.

The reverse is also true: a number below the threshold does not automatically mean starting medication. Your doctor will weigh your overall fracture risk with you.
</Callout>

## Who should be scanned

That is a decision for you and your doctor. It is generally considered in people with risk factors — increasing age, postmenopausal women, a previous fracture from a fall that should not have broken a bone, a parent who fractured a hip, long-term steroid use, smoking, or very low body weight.

If you are not sure whether it applies to you, ask your doctor rather than deciding from what you read online.

## Preparation and cautions

- Skip calcium supplements on the day of the scan; undissolved tablets can interfere with the image
- If you have recently had a test using contrast, such as a barium study or a CT scan, mention it — a gap may be needed
- Wear clothing without buttons, zips or metal hooks around the waist and hips
- **Always say if you are or might be pregnant**
- If you have been scanned before, use the same machine or centre if you can. Comparing across machines is much harder

## Questions to ask your doctor

<DoctorChecklist>
- What does my result mean, taken together with my age and history?
- Roughly what is my risk of fracture over the next ten years?
- Should I be starting medication, and if not, what should I be doing instead?
- How much calcium and vitamin D do I need, and from food or supplements?
- When should this be repeated?
</DoctorChecklist>
