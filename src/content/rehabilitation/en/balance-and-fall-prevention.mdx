<KeyFacts>
- Thin bone does not break on its own. What breaks it is a fall.
- Around 20–30% of people aged 65 and over fall at least once a year.
- The best-supported programmes combine balance and resistance training, supervised, over more than three months.
- Walking-focused programmes did not reach moderate-certainty evidence of benefit.
- Home safety, a medication review and an eye test are part of prevention too.
</KeyFacts>

## Why falls matter more than people think

Osteoporosis usually causes no symptoms at all until a bone breaks, and in real life the event that breaks it is almost always a fall. A fall from standing height rarely breaks a healthy bone; in thinner bone, the same force can be enough.

So looking after bone always has two halves: making the bone as strong as it can be, and **reducing the chance that you fall**. Doing only the first is doing half the job.

## What the evidence shows

A large systematic review and network meta-analysis of fall prevention in older adults living in the community pooled 219 randomised trials with more than 167,000 participants, and gives usable answers.

Interventions with moderate-certainty evidence of benefit over one to two years included:

- **Supervised balance and resistance training** lasting longer than three months
- **Group Tai Chi**
- Comprehensive risk assessment with a targeted plan, combined with a home-hazard assessment

And the reviewers state it plainly: **none of the interventions with moderate-certainty benefit was focused on walking**.

<Callout type="info" title="What people prefer matters too">
The same work reviewed which programmes older adults actually prefer. Balance and resistance training was clearly preferred over Tai Chi and other forms of exercise, and there was high-certainty evidence that individual delivery was preferred over group sessions for balance and resistance programmes.

This is not a minor point. The theoretically best programme that you do not want to do will not help you. If you have a preference, tell your doctor or physiotherapist.
</Callout>

## What the training involves

**Leg strength.** Standing up from a chair without using your hands is both an exercise and a test in itself.

**Static balance.** Standing with the feet in a line, or on one leg — always with something solid within reach.

**Balance while moving.** Shifting weight side to side, marching on the spot, stepping over imagined obstacles.

**Back strength and posture.** Particularly important for anyone with spinal compression fractures.

The [osteoporosis](/en/conditions/osteoporosis) article has illustrated exercises taken from the author's osteoporosis care app.

<Callout type="warning" title="Practise balance safely">
Balance exercises carry a risk of their own. Always work beside a kitchen counter, a wall or a sturdy chair back, so there is something to grab immediately. If you can, have someone with you at first.

If you have fallen before, feel light-headed, or take medication that makes you drowsy, start under the supervision of a physiotherapist rather than on your own at home.
</Callout>

## Beyond exercise

- **Review your medicines** with your doctor or pharmacist. Some cause drowsiness, dizziness, or a drop in blood pressure on standing.
- **Have your eyes checked.** Multifocal glasses can make it hard to judge the edge of a step.
- **Change the house.** Clear cables, loose mats and clutter from the floor. Put a light on the route to the bathroom. Fit grab rails where they help.
- **Footwear.** Low heels, non-slip soles and a fastening that holds the foot, rather than slip-on sandals.
- **Calcium and vitamin D** as advised by your doctor, which form part of osteoporosis care guidelines.

## Questions to ask your doctor

<DoctorChecklist>
- How high is my risk of falling, and what is driving it?
- Do any of my medicines increase that risk?
- What kind of programme should I start, and should it be supervised?
- Should I have a bone density scan, and when?
- What should I change at home first?
